Data journalism · Cities and cropland

When a city expands, what disappears beneath it?

Cities across India are growing outward over wheat, ragi and paddy, and this page follows what the evidence says about how much land goes, and what goes with it.

Run the land-loss explorer →

The story

The last furrow before the flyover

The plot described here is an illustrative composite, not a reported case.

Picture three acres at the end of a city bus route. For two generations the plot has been sown twice a year: wheat after the monsoon, a short pulse in the gap. Then the road is widened. A layout is approved. A surveyor drives wooden stakes into the headland, and nobody announces anything.

Cities do not take land all at once. They take it one plot at a time.

That is what makes the loss hard to see. A satellite records a speckle of bright roofs where last year the ground had the soft texture of a crop. A census reclassifies a village as a town. A farmer sees a buyer at the gate, an offer that is hard to refuse, and a canal that no longer reaches the tail end of the village.

Soil that took centuries to form is sealed in a season. Rain that once soaked into the field now runs off to a drain. The vegetables that fed a neighbourhood begin to arrive from farther away.

This page follows that edge. It starts with what the evidence can and cannot say about the scale of the loss. It then lets you run a simplified simulation for more than a hundred study regions, and ends by asking what a city could choose to do differently.

Quick orientation

How much, and how sure are we?

Three figures, each with its limits. Read the notes under each one, because the scope of a number decides what it can tell you.

1 in 4

Roughly one quarter of the new urban land in India's largest urban agglomerations between 1992 and 2012 was formerly cropland. Most of the rest came from woodland, shrub and grass cover.

Scope: 47 urban agglomerations with at least one million people in the 2011 census, 1992 to 2012. Measures: urban extent detected from night-time satellite light, compared with land-cover maps; the urban expansion trend was about 2.4% a year. It is not a national total. Source 1

68% → 15%

Vegetation cover in Bengaluru fell from about 68% of the study area in 1973 to under 15% in 2013, while built-up area rose from about 8% to about 74%.

Scope: one city, a study area of roughly 680 sq km (derived from the reported hectares and percentages). Measures: satellite land-cover classification, reported at 91.5% overall accuracy. Vegetation here includes trees, parks and farmland together, so it is not a cropland figure. Source 2

−0.5 Mha

Nationally, agricultural land slipped from 180.62 to 180.11 million hectares between 2018-19 and 2021-22, while total cropped area rose from 201.18 to 219.16 million hectares.

Scope: all of India, three years. Measures: official land-use statistics. Cropped area counts a plot each time it is sown, so multiple cropping lifts it. The totals hide local losses at city edges and cannot show where land was lost. Sources 3 and 4

      The regional ledger

      What grows where the city is heading

      Six landscapes, each defined by a crop and by the pressure it faces. The pressures come from planning and academic literature and are described in general terms. The simulation above does not model them individually.

      PunjabWheat and paddy

      The wheat belt

      What grows
      Wheat in winter and paddy in summer across the central plains, among the most intensively cropped land in the country.
      Pressure on the land
      Housing and industrial estates spreading from Mohali, Ludhiana and Jalandhar, on top of groundwater levels that have been falling across large parts of the state. That raises the cost of farming the land that remains. See the Central Ground Water Board assessments. Source 5
      KarnatakaRagi

      The ragi zones

      What grows
      Rain-fed finger millet across the southern plateau, grown with pulses and oilseeds on small dryland holdings.
      Pressure on the land
      Layouts and logistics parks reaching out from Bengaluru toward Tumakuru, Kolar and Ramanagara. Near the city, a dryland plot can be worth far more as a site than it earns as a farm.
      Tamil NaduPaddy

      The delta paddies

      What grows
      Kuruvai, samba and thaladi paddy seasons across the Cauvery delta around Thanjavur, Tiruvarur and Nagapattinam.
      Pressure on the land
      Irrigation that depends on river releases that vary from year to year makes farming risky, while highway corridors and town edges attract plotting.
      KeralaWetland paddy

      Kuttanad below the sea

      What grows
      Paddy farmed in low-lying fields and canals around Alappuzha, in a short season set by the water.
      Pressure on the land
      Pressure to fill land for buildings and roads. The Kerala Conservation of Paddy Land and Wetland Act, 2008 restricts conversion, though applications and enforcement remain contested.
      MaharashtraGrapes and onions

      The Nashik corridor

      What grows
      Vineyards and onion fields around Nashik, in the same band of country as the Mumbai-Pune-Nashik growth corridor.
      Pressure on the land
      Industrial estates, highway widening and housing along the corridor, with fringe farms facing steady offers from developers.
      West BengalRice and potato

      The Hooghly belt

      What grows
      Aman rice and potatoes on the fertile alluvial plains west of Kolkata.
      Pressure on the land
      Kolkata's outward growth through new townships and road links. The flat alluvial soils that make the belt productive are also easy to build on.

      Human and food-system impacts

      What the loss touches

      Some effects are well documented. Others are plausible but still being studied. Each topic below keeps the two apart.

      Established supported by direct evidence Possible plausible, still being studied

      Soil health

      Established

      Sealing a field under concrete or asphalt takes its soil out of production, and in practice the change is very hard to reverse. Topsoil is also stripped for bricks and fill around growing cities.

      Possible

      Farms at city edges may be tended less carefully while owners wait to sell, and fertility could decline. Research on this "waiting land" effect in India is limited.

      Water tables

      Established

      Paved surfaces let less rain soak in, so less water recharges groundwater beneath built-up land. In much of India, though, the larger driver of falling water tables is pumping for irrigation, not cities.

      Possible

      Building over recharge zones and village tanks could worsen local water scarcity and flooding together. How large the effect is depends heavily on where building happens.

      Local food production

      Established

      India's national cropped area has risen even as city edges have grown, helped by irrigation and multiple cropping. The losses are real but local.

      Possible

      Where conversion falls on the most productive land near cities, the vegetables, dairy and fruit that supply those cities may shift to farms farther away.

      Longer supply chains

      Established

      Food that travels farther needs more transport and often more cold storage, and both use fuel and electricity.

      Possible

      The net change in emissions depends on vehicle type, load and spoilage rates. Transport is only one part of a food product's footprint, so the size of this effect for India is uncertain.

      Sustainable planning choices

      What a city can choose

      Three approaches that planners use to grow without consuming as much farmland. Each helps in some places and backfires in others.

      Compact, vertical growth

      How it helps
      Housing more people on each hectare leaves more land at the edge untouched, and shorter trips can cut travel emissions.
      The trade-off
      Taller buildings need water, drainage, transit and open space to match, and dense neighbourhoods can trap heat without shade and green cover.

      Greenbelts and growth boundaries

      How it helps
      A protected ring of farmland or open space around a city holds the edge in place and keeps nearby fields in production.
      The trade-off
      If housing demand is not met inside the ring, building can leapfrog past it and lengthen commutes. Landowners inside the belt also need fair treatment.

      Protecting multi-crop land

      How it helps
      Ranking land by irrigation and cropping intensity lets a city steer growth toward poorer land and away from fields that give several harvests a year. Kerala's paddy and wetland law is one example.
      The trade-off
      Restrictions lower what farmers can earn from their plots unless they are paid for protecting them, and rules that are weakly enforced protect little.

      A note on context

      None of these is a rule that works everywhere. A hill town, a delta city and a plains metropolis differ in terrain, water, land tenure and local politics. The right mix depends on the place, and it needs to be tested against local evidence before it is copied from somewhere else.

      Methodology of the simulation

      Each study region is assigned a growth class. The model estimates its built-up share of the study window in a given year as:

      built(year) = min(55%, s0 × (1 + g)^(year − 2000))

      Here s0 is the starting share in 2000 and g is the yearly growth rate, both set by the class and nudged by up to 10% using a fixed value derived from the region's name, so the same region always gives the same result. Converted land is built(end) − built(start), multiplied by the window area. The share of mapped land lost is converted land divided by the window area.

      Simulation boundaries and limits of precision

      The growth classes, window sizes and rates are illustrative assumptions chosen for teaching. They are not measurements, and they have not been calibrated against satellite imagery for any region. The map outlines are generated shapes, not boundaries. All unbuilt land is drawn as cultivated, while the cited research shows that new urban land comes from several land covers, only about a quarter of it from cropland in the largest agglomerations.

      Read the output as an order-of-magnitude illustration. A real estimate would classify satellite imagery, such as Landsat or Sentinel-2, and validate it on the ground.
